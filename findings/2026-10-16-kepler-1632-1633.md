# Update, 2026-10-16 (fourth): Kepler-1632 b's default period is twice the real one (as Coughlin et al. 2016 already noted), and Kepler's data cannot fix Kepler-1633 b's period

By MV2 of Munim, Inc. The code, queries, checks and this write-up were made with AI agents working in his name. No person has re-derived these numbers yet. Text: CC BY 4.0; code Apache-2.0.

## Correction (2026-10-16, about 18:45 UTC): Kepler-1632 b's halved period is not new

The first version of this file said we had found no paper testing Kepler-1632 b's period. That was wrong. **Coughlin et al. 2016** (the Q1–Q17 DR24 KOI catalogue paper, ApJS 224, 12; arXiv:1512.06149) covered it in **§V.5.4, "KOI 5475.01"**. The Q1–Q16 search found the signal at 448 days and marked it a false positive because of an apparent secondary eclipse. The DR24 search then found it at 224 days. By eye, the authors saw no odd–even difference, and they concluded that the Q1–Q16 detection "was at twice the true orbital period". They also wrote that the DR24 catalogue's insolation of 0.68 S⊕ "is too low" and "should actually be 1.71 S⊕". In their Table 7 of possible habitable-zone candidates, KOI-5475.01 carries note b, "Modeled at twice the true orbital period."

So the period itself was known to be wrong in 2016. What this file still adds:

- **An epoch-by-epoch light-curve check** (section 2). All six 224.15 d epochs are deep, including the three that the 448.3 d ephemeris says should be empty. Coughlin et al. reported an odd–even inspection, not per-epoch depths.
- **The fix never reached the default.** Ten years on, the NASA Exoplanet Archive's default row (Morton et al. 2016) still gives 448.303558 d, and so do NASA's Exoplanet Catalog, the Open Exoplanet Catalogue, Wikidata and ExoKyoto. The Archive's stored Q1–Q16 and DR24 KOI rows still give 448.30 d too, although the DR24 catalogue paper itself says that value is wrong.
- **A composite row that mixes the two periods** (section 2).

The Kepler-1633 b part (section 3) is unchanged. We still found no paper that ties its period choice to its alternate-quarter coverage. We read the text of Coughlin et al. 2016, Thompson et al. 2018 (DR25) and Lissauer et al. 2024 for KOI-5568, but not Lissauer et al.'s machine-readable table.

This file adds to `findings/2026-10-16-lightcurves.md` and `findings/2026-10-16-kepler-1301.md`. It reports two sandboxed census runs: `kepler_alias.py` 0.2.0 (results in `results/2026-10-16-kepler-alias-0.2.0/`) and `depth_census.py` 0.1.0 (results in `results/2026-10-16-depth-census-0.1.0/`). Both ran on 2026-10-16 around 16:00 UTC on live NASA Exoplanet Archive tables and Kepler light curves from MAST. Each result below was then checked by hand against the Archive's tables.

## 2. Kepler-1632 b (KOI-5475.01, KIC 8105398): the Archive's default period is twice the real one

The Archive's census compares each confirmed Kepler planet's default period with its DR25 KOI period. It found two pairs, both from Morton et al. 2016: Kepler-1632 b and Kepler-1633 b. For Kepler-1632 b, Coughlin et al. 2016 had already given the right period (see the correction above).

| source | period (d) |
|---|---|
| Q1–Q16 and DR24 KOI tables | 448.303558 |
| Morton et al. 2016 (the Archive's default, `default_flag` = 1) | 448.303558 |
| DR25 KOI table, DR25 TCE, and the Archive's cumulative table | 224.150923 (6 transits) |
| Coughlin et al. 2016, §V.5.4 (text) | 224 ("twice the true orbital period") |

The star was observed in every quarter (18 long-cadence files, Q0–Q17). We folded the light curve on the 224.15 d DR25 ephemeris. **There is a transit at every one of the six epochs.** Each has 7 to 18 in-transit cadences, with the star's other KOI (5.68 d) masked:

| epoch (224.15 d) | mid-time (BKJD) | predicted by the 448.3 d ephemeris? | depth (ppm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 341.03 | no | 330 ± 30 |
| 1 | 565.18 | yes (Morton's T0 = BKJD 565.176) | 400 ± 40 |
| 2 | 789.33 | no | 295 ± 34 |
| 3 | 1013.48 | yes | 460 ± 28 |
| 4 | 1237.63 | no | 344 ± 29 |
| 5 | 1461.78 | yes | 319 ± 30 |

At 448.3 d, epochs 0, 2 and 4 should be flat. Each is instead 9–12σ deep. The two groups average 326 ± 18 and 394 ± 44 ppm, 1.4σ apart. **The real period is 224.15 d** (or a whole fraction of it; DR25's search did not find one). This agrees with Coughlin et al. 2016.

The composite row already mixes the two periods. It pairs P = 448.30 d (Morton et al. 2016) with a = 0.6762 au and Teq = 369 K from the DR25 KOI fit at 224.15 d, and with Berger et al. 2018's insolation of 6.06 S⊕. Kepler's law with the composite's own 1.12 M☉ gives 1.19 au at 448.3 d (stored a / Kepler a = 0.568). A user who believes the period would place the planet at 1.19 au and about 296 K, a temperate sub-Neptune. The real orbit is about 0.75 au, at about 373 K (zero albedo, composite star: 1.19 R☉, 6137 K). The NASA Exoplanet Catalog page, the Open Exoplanet Catalogue, Wikidata and ExoKyoto all give 448.3 d.

One caveat: an eclipsing binary at 448.3 d with two equal eclipses at phase 0.5 would look the same. That is the same caveat as for Kepler-1604 b and Kepler-1301 b. Morton et al. validated the planet with the 448.3 d ephemeris as input.

## 3. Kepler-1633 b (KOI-5568.01, KIC 8745553): Kepler's data cannot choose between 62.13, 93.20 and 186.40 d

| source | period (d) |
|---|---|
| Q1–Q16 and DR24 KOI tables; Morton et al. 2016 (default); Holczer et al. 2016; Gajdoš et al. 2019 | 186.404 |
| DR25 KOI table, DR25 TCE and cumulative table (8 transits) | 62.134919 |

**This star was observed only in the even quarters.** MAST has long-cadence files for Q2, Q4, Q6, Q8, Q10, Q12, Q14 and Q16 only, and the Archive's Kepler stellar table gives `st_quarters` = `01010101010101010`. A quarter lasts about 93 d, so the data come in roughly 93-day blocks, one every ~186 d. Three times 62.135 d is 186.40 d, so the 62.13 d epochs keep the same phase relative to that cycle.

With the DR25 ephemeris (T0 = BKJD 154.0853), epochs 1, 4, 7, …, 22 fall at BKJD 216.2, 402.6, 589.0, 775.4, 961.8, 1148.2, 1334.6 and 1521.1, one per even quarter from Q2 to Q16. Every other 62.13 d epoch (n = 2, 3, 5, 6, …) falls in an odd quarter, where there are no data. The quarter assignment uses approximate quarter boundaries; the job in section 5 re-checks it against the actual cadences.

- `kepler_alias` 0.2.0 found usable data at 7 epochs, numbers 4, 7, …, 22, all of the same group mod 3. The data give 255, 81, 79, 263, 186, 193 and 97 ppm (errors 39–60 ppm), a mean of 149 ± 28 ppm. The two other groups had **no data at all**, so the test returned "inconclusive". DR25 counts 8 transits at 62.13 d (`koi_num_transits`), the number that 186.4 d gives with one transit in each of the 8 observed quarters.
- Every period that only adds epochs in unobserved quarters fits the data equally well. 93.20 d (186.4/2) adds one transit a quarter after each observed one, again in an odd quarter, so it is also allowed. Shorter divisors such as 46.6 d appear to put extra transits inside observed quarters (for example 402.62 − 46.60 = 356.0, early in Q4); the coverage job will check this.
- The transit duration does not settle it either. For the 1.18 M☉, 1.84 R☉ star in the DR25 supplemental and Gajdoš rows, the longest central transit is 12.5 h at 62.13 d, 14.4 h at 93.20 d and 18.1 h at 186.40 d. The measured 8.3–10.5 h fits each of them with a moderate to high impact parameter.
- **TESS is unlikely to help.** The cumulative table gives a 147.5 ppm depth, 8.7 h duration and Kepler magnitude 13.4. For a star this faint, TESS's photometric noise is of order 1,000 ppm in an hour, so one transit is detected at well under 1σ. Even five or six transits from the Kepler field's TESS sectors would stay near 1σ. This is an order-of-magnitude estimate; we did not check which sectors observed KIC 8745553.

So **Kepler's light curve does not decide between the DR25 period and the default period.** The default is not shown to be wrong. But the composite row is inconsistent, as for Kepler-1632 b. It pairs P = 186.40 d with a = 0.3875 au and Teq = 925 K from the DR25 KOI fit, which used 62.13 d and a 2.0 M☉, 4.9 R☉ star. With the composite's own star (1.20 M☉, 1.32 R☉, 6256 K), 186.40 d gives a ≈ 0.679 au and Teq ≈ 421 K (stored a / Kepler a = 0.571). A radial-velocity orbit, or much more precise photometry than TESS gives at this brightness, would be needed to choose.

## 4. depth_census 0.1.0: no new paper-checked case this time

The census compares each row's three radius-ratio estimates: `pl_ratror`, the square root of the depth, and Rp/R★. Across 20,875 `ps` rows and 4,642 composite rows it flagged 3 + 22 in the radius-ratio class, 236 + 169 in the depth class and 2 + 113 in the radius class. The rest were two-way, mixed or grazing. The leading flags are cases already in FINDINGS.md: HAT-P-25 b, K2-114 b, TOI-1478 b, TOI-5624 c, GJ 3929 b, the Hedges et al. 2019 K2 planets, TOI-2048 b, the Guenther et al. 2026 pair, and Kepler-359 d (explained earlier). One new lead was explained by hand:

- **TOI-2267 d (composite): explained, not an error.** The composite's depth (0.114 %) is ExoFOP's, computed for the brighter star of this binary. Its `pl_ratror` (0.121) and radius are Greklek-McKeon et al. 2026's, for the companion host (R★ = 0.13 R☉). The depth is diluted and the ratio is not, so the two disagree.

Still unchecked: K2-100 b (composite depth from Barragán et al. 2019 is 0.15× the ratio squared) and the long tail of single-source depth-class rows from the KOI tables (mostly grazing or limb-darkened fits).

## 5. Queued next: a census of every Kepler period that Kepler's coverage cannot pin down

Kepler-1633 b suggests a test that needs no light curves for most stars. Take each star's observed quarters (`st_quarters`) and the times of Kepler's data stretches in each quarter. Then ask, for every non-false-positive DR25 KOI and every confirmed planet whose default period differs from its KOI period:

- **P/k (k = 2–5):** are any of the extra epochs of P/k covered? If none are, Kepler's data cannot exclude P/k.
- **k·P:** do all the covered epochs have the same epoch number mod k? If they do, Kepler's data cannot exclude k·P.

Each flagged row is then re-tested on the star's own cadences. `kepler_coverage.py` 0.1.0 does this. It and `kepler_alias.py` 0.3.0 (which now also tests the 18 DR25 candidates in the alias census, and adds the odd–even statistic and the transit-count check) are queued as sandbox jobs.

Predictions, written before the run:
- Kepler-1633 b is flagged 3P on its DR25 ephemeris, and P/2 and P/3 (not P/4 or P/5) on the default 186.40 d ephemeris.
- Kepler-1632 b and Kepler-1802 b are not flagged on either ephemeris.
- Fewer than 25 confirmed planets carry any P/k or k·P flag.
- At least 80 % of the light-curve re-tests agree with the quarter-based flags.

Added at the correction: one DR25 candidate in the queued candidate run, KOI-521.02, is already settled in print. Lissauer et al. 2024 (§II.3) give its period as 5.41 d, half the 10.82 d of earlier catalogues. If the run agrees, that confirms a published result; it is not a new one.

## Prior art

Searched on 2026-10-16 for "Kepler-1632 b", "KOI-5475", "Kepler-1633 b", "KOI-5568", and for Kepler period aliases caused by data gaps. The DR24, DR25 and Lissauer et al. papers were read in full text at about 18:30 UTC.
- **Coughlin et al. 2016** (ApJS 224, 12; arXiv:1512.06149), §V.5.4 and Table 7, note b: KOI-5475.01's 448-day period is twice the true one. This came from the DR24 catalogue team's own inspection. We missed it in the first version of this file.
- Thompson et al. 2018 (DR25; arXiv:1710.06758), §VI: TCEs at double or half a KOI's period are federated to the KOI. We found no mention of KOI-5568 or of factor-of-three changes.
- Lissauer et al. 2024 (Planetary Science Journal 5, no. 6; arXiv:2311.00238) checked whether data gaps could hide transits for the 10 two-transit candidates with P > 730 d; only KOI-375.01 could be at half its period. They halved KOI-521.02's period and corrected aliases in some multi-planet systems. Their text does not mention KOI-5475 or KOI-5568; we have not read their machine-readable table.
- Kipping et al. 2014 excluded the half-period alias of Kepler-421 b this way.

As far as we found, no one has published a census of all Kepler KOIs whose periods Kepler's own coverage cannot pin down, or tied Kepler-1633 b's period choice to its alternate-quarter coverage.

## Status

Kepler-1632 b: the period was corrected in print by Coughlin et al. 2016, and the Archive's default row has not caught up. Our light curve supports the 224.15 d period. Kepler-1633 b is a documented ambiguity, not an error claim. Neither has been sent to the Archive; a person checks first.
